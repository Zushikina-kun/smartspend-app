===== [0051] (normal) =====
Financial mismanagement remained a critical and documented challenge among Filipino households, compounded by limited access to accessible, localized, and intelligent financial tools. This study aimed to: (1) assess the financial management practices, budgeting challenges, and expense-tracking behaviors of parents aged 35–55 and young professionals aged 21–35 in San Fernando City, La Union; (2) design and develop SmartSpend, an AI-assisted mobile financial tracking and advisory application for Android; and (3) evaluate the usability of the developed system using the System Usability Scale (SUS).

===== [0052] (normal) =====
The study employed a mixed-methods developmental research design. A validated survey questionnaire was administered to assess financial practices and technology acceptance. SmartSpend was developed using an Agile Kanban methodology, integrating a multi-provider large language model (LLM) architecture with Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite as the primary model, enabling 34 autonomous financial management actions. Usability was evaluated using the 10-item Brooke (1996) SUS instrument administered after respondents used the application for at least 10 minutes.

===== [0053] (normal) =====
The needs assessment revealed that 96.7% of respondents expressed positive intent to use an AI-powered financial application, and 93.3% already used GCash, validating the target features. SmartSpend version 3.1.0 was successfully developed as an offline-first Android application with multi-modal expense input, a Financial Health Score (FHS) formally classified as a Prototype Observed Financial Health Indicator, and a six-provider AI failover chain. Usability evaluation yielded a mean SUS score of 61.83 (SD = 17.58), corresponding to a grade of D and an adjective rating of "OK" under the Bangor et al. (2009) scale below the 68-point benchmark (Sauro & Lewis, 2016) and the pre-established target of 80.

===== [0054] (normal) =====
The results indicated that SmartSpend showed promise as a prototype AI-assisted financial tool for the target demographic, though the below-target usability score highlighted areas for UI/UX refinement. The study contributed a locally contextualized, evaluated prototype that addressed gaps in existing Philippine personal finance applications, particularly the absence of Filipino-English LLM integration, offline-first operation, and behavioral health scoring. Seven recommendations were identified for future development, including UI/UX refinement, a backend API proxy, on-device AI, SQLite encryption, formal parsing evaluation, and a longitudinal study.

This chapter presented the background of the study, including the context and motivation for developing the SmartSpend mobile application. It also discussed the objectives of the research, scope and limitations of the system, and its intended purpose and users. ===== [0177] (normal) =====
Several existing applications demonstrated how mobile tools could support personal financial management but also revealed gaps that SmartSpend intended to address. Tarsi – Budget Tracker, a recently introduced application available in the Philippine App Store, supported offline expense tracking, receipt image capture, and category-based summaries, which made it suitable for users who preferred manual or semi-automatic tracking (Tarsi – Budget Tracker, 2026). However, it did not provide a conversational assistant that could interpret natural language commands, nor did it compute a unified financial health score that reflected overall budgeting behavior. YNAB (You Need A Budget) implemented a zero-based envelope budgeting method that was effective for debt payoff planning, but required manual entry and a subscription cost that might have been less accessible to Filipino users (Yomio, 2026). Copilot provided AI-based expense categorization but was limited to iOS and did not support Filipino-English voice or OCR input. PocketGuard simplified available spending into a single daily figure but lacked spending behavior analysis, health scoring, or multi-modal input. These comparisons demonstrated the value of existing digital financial tools but confirmed that there was still room for a localized solution that combined offline capability, multi-modal expense input, and an accessible behavioral scoring mechanism. ===== [0209] (normal) =====
The system had several limitations. The performance of OCR text recognition depended on the quality and clarity of receipt images, and handwritten or low-quality images might produce inaccurate results. Voice recognition accuracy will be affected by background noise and device support for the Filipino English (en_PH) locale. To address these issues, manual input options remained available after using OCR, barcode scanning, or voice input features. All AI-powered features, including expense parsing, financial insights, and the conversational assistant, required an active internet connection, as they relied on an external LLM API service. The application did not support direct banking integration. Bank synchronization was excluded due to the absence of accessible open-banking APIs in the Philippine setting and the data privacy implications of transmitting banking credentials to a third-party service. Investment features were outside scope as the target population primarily required support for day-to-day expense control and savings discipline rather than investment optimization. The application did not provide professional financial or tax advice. Any financial estimates generated by the system were approximations based on simplified models. Market price estimates provided by the AI assistant were based on the language model's training data and optional web search results, and might not reflect real-time pricing. Users were advised to verify prices through official sources before making financial decisions. Additionally, profile images were stored locally on the device and were not synchronized across devices due to limitations of the Firebase Spark (free) plan. The system was designed within the constraints of free-tier services and remained limited to Android platform compatibility.

Technical Background

===== [0221] (normal) =====
In this part, the researchers described the technologies that were used during the design and development of the SmartSpend mobile application. These were critical to making the implementation of features described above possible.

===== [0222] (normal) =====
Flutter. Flutter was an open-source UI framework developed by Google that was used to build the SmartSpend Android application. It provided a rich set of widgets and tools that enabled the development of a responsive, visually consistent, and feature-rich mobile interface (Google, 2024f).

===== [0223] (normal) =====
SQLite. The application used SQLite as its primary local database via the sqflite package (version 11), storing all financial data including expenses, budgets, savings goals, income, recurring transactions, debts, and chat history directly on the device. Expenses were categorized across 14 expense types  including Food, Transportation, Gaming, Personal Care, Clothing, Gifts, and Travel  and tagged by payment method, supporting GCash, Maya, GrabPay, ShopeePay, debit and credit cards, and bank transfers. This ensured full offline functionality without requiring an internet connection (Roux, 2019).

===== [0224] (normal) =====
Firebase Firestore. Firebase Firestore was used for bidirectional cloud synchronization of user financial data. Every write operation was automatically pushed to Firestore, and upon login, the application retrieved cloud data and merged it into the local database, enabling seamless multi-device access to financial records (Google, 2024c).

===== [0225] (normal) =====
Firebase Authentication. Firebase Authentication handled user identity management, supporting email/password login, Google Sign-In via OAuth 2.0, and biometric authentication (fingerprint or face unlock) as a convenience layer after the user's first successful login (Google, 2024a).

===== [0226] (normal) =====
Firebase Crashlytics. Firebase Crashlytics was integrated for real-time crash reporting. It captured Flutter framework errors and asynchronous exceptions and reported them to the Firebase Console for monitoring and debugging (Google, 2024b).

===== [0227] (normal) =====
LLM API Integration. The system integrated a large language model (LLM) API as its AI engine, selected through a comparative technical evaluation of available providers based on inference speed, response accuracy for Filipino-English natural language processing, context window capacity, and cost feasibility for academic deployment. At the time of writing, Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite (Google) was the primary model, backed by seven automatic fallback providers spanning Google, Groq, and Cerebras so the assistant kept working if any single provider was unavailable or rate-limited. 

===== [0228] (normal) =====
Speech-to-Text. The speech_to_text package was used to implement voice input for expense recording. The system automatically detected the Filipino English (en_PH) locale, with a fallback to en_US, and supported a 15-second listening window with automatic pause detection (Sloane, 2022). 

===== [0229] (normal) =====
Google ML Kit Text Recognition (OCR). Google ML Kit’s Latin script text recognizer was used to extract text from receipt images. The extracted text was cleaned, trimmed, and processed through an intelligent routing mechanism. For single-item receipts, the extracted text was passed directly to the AI chat interface for natural language parsing and automatic expense recording. For multi-item receipts, the system detected multiple line entries and routed the data to a dedicated import review screen, where each item was parsed individually using a specialized receipt parsing function. Users could review extracted entries, edit categories, assign Want or Need classifications, and selectively import items before confirming. This dual-flow design improved usability by supporting both quick single-entry recording and efficient bulk transaction processing from detailed receipts. Users had the option to capture a new image or select one from the gallery (Google, 2024e).

===== [0230] (normal) =====
Mobile Scanner (Barcode). The mobile_scanner package was used for barcode scanning. Each scan was saved to a local scan history, and a prompt asked the user to describe the product before AI parsing. The scanner included a torch toggle and a visual overlay guide frame (Bociek, 2023; ZXing, 2023).

===== [0231] (normal) =====
Backup and Restore. The application implemented backup and restore functionality through the system share sheet. All financial data including expenses, budgets, savings goals, income, recurring transactions, debts, wallet balances, mood log, and installment plans was exported as a structured JSON file (version 9 format) that could be shared via any installed application or saved to local storage. Restoration was performed by selecting a previously exported JSON backup file. This approach ensured data portability without requiring additional cloud service dependencies beyond the existing Firebase synchronization.

===== [0232] (normal) =====
fl_chart. The fl_chart package was used to render interactive charts in the Analytics screen, including a pie chart for category breakdown, a bar chart for monthly spending trends, and a line chart for the 30-day Financial Health Score history (Flutter 4 Fun, 2022).

===== [0233] (normal) =====
local_auth. The local_auth package enabled biometric authentication (fingerprint or face unlock) as a login option after the user’s first successful login, using the device’s enrolled biometrics with a PIN fallback (The Flutter Authors, 2013).

===== [0234] (normal) =====
Financial Health Score (FHS). SmartSpend introduced a Financial Health Score ranging from 0 to 100 that provided users with a single, interpretable indicator of their budgeting behavior. The score operated in two modes depending on the user's settings. In Full Mode (income tracking enabled), it was computed from four equally weighted components: Savings Rate, Overspend Control, Budget Adherence, and Logging Consistency each contributing 25% to the total score. In Lightweight Mode (income tracking disabled), the four components were: Spending Restraint (vs. a user-defined spending limit), Logging Consistency, Category Balance (preventing any single category from dominating spending), and Habit Streak (consecutive days of expense logging). This dual-mode design made the system usable for individuals who did not have a fixed income, such as students and informal workers, without penalizing them for not setting income data. The detailed formula, component weights, and consequence mechanism were fully documented in Chapter 3: Results and Discussion.

===== [0303] (normal) =====
The selection of an appropriate LLM API was a critical design decision because it directly influenced the accuracy, latency, and cost of natural language expense parsing and conversational assistance. For a mobile financial assistant requiring Filipino-English capability and free-tier deployment, the evaluation criteria were weighted as: Filipino-English accuracy (25%), speed/latency (20%), tool use and JSON reliability (20%), free tier availability (15%), context window (10%), and financial reasoning quality (10%). Table 3.2 presented the comparative evaluation results.

===== [0304] (normal) =====
Table 3.2. Comparative Evaluation of LLM APIs for SmartSpend Integration

===== [0305] (normal) =====
Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite was selected as the primary model because it offered a high free-tier request quota, good Filipino-English (Taglish) handling in the team trials, a 1-million token context window, and native function calling support for the 34 agentic action types (Li et al., 2024; Google, 2024f). Groq's LLaMA-family endpoints (LLaMA 4 Scout, LLaMA 3.3 70B, LLaMA 3.1 8B) were retired by Groq in mid-to-late 2026 and had been replaced in the fallback chain with Groq-hosted OpenAI GPT-OSS (120B/20B) and Qwen (3.6/3.8 27B) models, which Groq then recommended as the direct migration path. Models like GPT-4o and Claude were paid-only cost-prohibitive for academic deployment at zero budget.

===== [0306] (normal) =====
SmartSpend used dynamic full-context injection rather than RAG. A typical user had 20–50 expenses, 5–10 budgets, and 3–5 goals (~1,000–5,000 tokens), fitting within any evaluated model's context window. RAG added unnecessary vector search overhead for this small per-user dataset (Davenport & Mittal, 2022).

===== [0307] (normal) =====
The Financial Health Score (FHS) was SmartSpend's core academic contribution formally positioned as a Prototype Observed Financial Health Indicator (FHI), adapting ideas from two published financial-health measurement efforts rather than inventing a scoring system from nothing. The main design choice computing the score from a user's own transaction data instead of a survey followed the Commonwealth Bank of Australia and Melbourne Institute's (CBA-MI) "Observed" scale, which summed categorical bank-record measures and rescaled the total to 0–100 (Comerton-Forde et al., 2018). Three of the four components below adapted the Spend, Save and Plan ideas from the Financial Health Network's FinHealth Score® (Financial Health Network, 2021). The UNSGSA's own working-group note on measuring financial health treated transaction/account-based measurement as a promising but still early-stage fourth approach, better viewed as complementary to survey-based scales rather than a full replacement (Gubbins, Mazzotta & Rhyne, 2021) which was exactly why this score was named a "Prototype," not presented as a validated instrument in its own right.

===== [0308] (normal) =====
Equal weights that added up to 100 followed the simplest published scoring method for this kind of scale. CBA's own 10-question Reported scale gave each question equal weight (0–4 points, 40 total) and multiplied by 2.5 to land on a 0–100 range, and separate validation testing found that this simple approach correlated very highly with more complex model-based scores (Gubbins, Mazzotta & Rhyne, 2021). SmartSpend followed the same logic with four components: 4 × 25 = 100. The weights themselves were a design choice, not a statistically derived result this was stated plainly rather than implied to be more rigorous than it was.

===== [0309] (normal) =====
Full Mode (income tracking enabled; 4 components of 25 points, 100 maximum):

===== [0310] (normal) =====
1. Savings Rate (25 pts): Score = 25 × min(1.0, savingsRate / 0.20). The 20% target came from the 50/30/20 budgeting rule (Warren & Tyagi, 2005).

===== [0311] (normal) =====
pushes spending over the daily budget counts as a "soft" overspend day at half weight; a day with scattered, habitual overspending across multiple items counts as "hard" at full weight.

===== [0312] (normal) =====
3. Budget Adherence (25 pts): Score = 25 × (onBudgetCategories / totalBudgetedCategories). Category-level budget limits followed the common personal-finance practice of zero-based budgeting (popularized by Ramsey, 2003), though this practice itself was not independently validated in peer-reviewed literature. The behavior of this component when zero category budgets were configured was a known open item pending confirmation against the current build and was not asserted here.

===== [0313] (normal) =====
4. Logging Consistency (25 pts): Score = 25 × (loggedDays / activeDays). Consistent self-monitoring increased transaction salience and promoted deliberate spending (Thaler & Sunstein, 2008).

Determined the Usability of SmartSpend Using the System Usability Scale 

===== [0352] (normal) =====
Respondents were required to use the SmartSpend application either the full version or the built-in Demo Mode accessible from the login screen for a minimum of 10 minutes before completing the SUS questionnaire. This was enforced through a screening question at the start of the SUS form. Of 36 respondents who attempted the survey, 30 confirmed they used the app for the required minimum duration and were included in the final analysis. The remaining 6 were excluded: 5 for insufficient app usage time and 1 for straight-line response bias. The respondents for the needs assessment (Objective 1) and for the SUS evaluation (Objective 3) were separate groups; some individuals may have taken part in both, but the two samples were analyzed independently for their respective objectives.

===== [0353] (normal) =====
SUS scores were computed using the standard formula: odd-numbered items minus 1; 5 minus even-numbered items; sum multiplied by 2.5 (Brooke, 1996). The overall SUS score was interpreted against the Bangor, Kortum, and Miller (2009) adjective rating scale.

Conclusions

===== [0372] (normal) =====


===== [0373] (normal) =====
SmartSpend v3.1.0 was developed as a working Android application with multi-modal expense input, offline-first local storage, 34 AI actions, and a Financial Health Score formally classified as a Prototype Observed Financial Health Indicator. The comparative evaluation of 20 candidate LLM models led to the selection of Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite as the primary model, supported by a multi-provider failover chain. Therefore, the second objective of designing and developing the SmartSpend mobile application, including the selection of a suitable LLM API, was achieved.

Recommendations

===== [0376] (normal) =====
In view of the results of the study, the following recommendations were made:

===== [0377] (normal) =====
1. Payday-cycle-aware budgeting. The researchers recommended that the developers implement budgeting resets aligned with the semi-monthly (15th and 30th) salary cycle common in the Philippines.

===== [0378] (normal) =====
2. Backend API proxy and per-user keys. The researchers recommended that the developers move LLM API key management to a server-side proxy (e.g., Firebase Cloud Function) to remove device-side key exposure and enforce per-user message limits, because the free-tier request quotas (Table 3.2) were shared by every user of the prototype. Letting users add their own key was an interim measure.

===== [0379] (normal) =====
3. Google Play release. The researchers recommended that, after the backend proxy and a privacy policy were in place, the developers submit  SmartSpend to the Google Play Store, since the prototype was currently distributed as a downloadable APK.

===== [0380] (normal) =====
4. On-device AI tier. The researchers recommended that the developers add  an opt-in on-device model (e.g., Gemma 4 E2B through Google's LiteRT-LM, about a 2.6 GB download) for users who wanted fully offline AI, routing only simple logging tasks to it and keeping complex financial advice on the cloud tier, because the current local AI mode required a separate computer on the same network.

===== [0381] (normal) =====
5. SQLite encryption. The researchers recommended that the developers implement SQLCipher-based encryption for the local database in a future schema migration, because the local database of the prototype was not encrypted.

===== [0382] (normal) =====
6. Household and remittance features. The researchers recommended that the developers add  shared couple and family wallets and inbound OFW remittance tracking as a distinct income category to serve more user groups.

===== [0383] (normal) =====
7. Longitudinal evaluation. The researchers recommended that future researchers measure  SmartSpend's effect on financial behavior over three to six months with a larger sample, because the usability evaluation in this study measured usability at a single point in time.
