ABSTRACT
Financial mismanagement remained a critical and documented challenge among Filipino households, compounded by limited access to accessible, localized, and intelligent financial tools. This study aimed to: (1) assess the financial management practices, budgeting challenges, and expense-tracking behaviors of parents aged 35–55 and young professionals aged 21–35 in San Fernando City, La Union; (2) design and develop SmartSpend, an AI-assisted mobile financial tracking and advisory application for Android; and (3) evaluate the usability of the developed system using the System Usability Scale (SUS).
The study employed a mixed-methods, developmental research design. Purposive sampling was used to select 30 respondents from the target population. A validated survey questionnaire was administered to assess financial practices and technology acceptance. SmartSpend was developed using an Agile Kanban methodology, integrating a multi-provider large language model (LLM) architecture with Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite as the primary model, enabling 34 autonomous financial management actions. Usability was evaluated using the 10-item Brooke (1996) SUS instrument administered after respondents used the application for at least 10 minutes.
The needs assessment revealed that 96.7% of respondents expressed positive intent to use an AI-powered financial application, and 93.3% already used GCash, validating the target features. SmartSpend version 3.1.0 was successfully developed as an offline-first Android application with multi-modal expense input, a Financial Health Score (FHS) formally classified as a Prototype Observed Financial Health Indicator, and a six-provider AI failover chain. Usability evaluation yielded a mean SUS score of 61.83 (SD = 17.58), corresponding to a grade of D and an adjective rating of "OK" under the Bangor et al. (2009) scale — below the 68-point benchmark (Sauro & Lewis, 2016) and the pre-established target of 80.
The results indicated that SmartSpend showed promise as a prototype AI-assisted financial tool for the target demographic, though the below-target usability score highlighted areas for UI/UX refinement. The study contributed a locally contextualized, evaluated prototype that addressed gaps in existing Philippine personal finance applications, particularly the absence of Filipino-English LLM integration, offline-first operation, and behavioral health scoring. Seven recommendations were identified for future development, including UI/UX refinement, a backend API proxy, on-device AI, SQLite encryption, formal parsing evaluation, and a longitudinal study.

Chapter I
INTRODUCTION
This chapter presented the background of the study, including the context and motivation for developing the SmartSpend mobile application. It also discussed the objectives of the research, scope and limitations of the system, and its intended purpose and users. In addition, this chapter outlined the conceptual framework that guided the system design, as well as the technical background of the technologies used in the development of the proposed AI-powered financial assistant.
Project Context
Financial mismanagement was a documented and pressing problem in the Philippines. According to the 2021 Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas (BSP) Financial Inclusion Survey, only 2% of Filipino adults correctly answered all six basic financial literacy questions — covering numeracy, interest rates, inflation, and risk — while fewer than half reported maintaining any household or personal budget (Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas, 2021). The Philippine Statistics Authority Family Income and Expenditure Survey (PSA FIES, 2021) further revealed that a substantial proportion of Filipino households spent beyond their monthly income, with recurring expenditures on food, transportation, and utilities consistently outpacing savings. In Filipino households, parents aged 35 to 55 typically served as primary financial decision-makers, managing budgeting, bill payments, and daily expenses, yet national survey data indicated that many adults still lack formal financial accounts and did not maintain written budgets, leaving household finances managed without structured tools or guidance (Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas, 2021; Philippine Statistics Authority, 2021). Despite high mobile phone penetration in the country and growing global mobile app adoption (Statista, 2024), most Filipinos did not use structured financial management tools, citing manual effort, poor local language support, and lacked of meaningful feedback as primary barriers to adoption (BSP, 2021; World Bank, 2022). Secondary analyses estimated that only around one in four Filipino adults met international financial literacy benchmarks — far below neighboring countries such as Malaysia and Singapore where rates exceeded 50% (Inquiro, 2024).
More recent data suggested this picture was beginning to shift, though unevenly. BSP's newer, annual Consumer Finance and Inclusion Survey (CFIS) 2025 reported that 74% of Filipinos now correctly answered basic financial literacy questions, up from 69% in 2021, alongside a reported decline in informal borrowing (Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas, 2025). The same survey reported formal account ownership actually fell to 50% of adults, down from 56% in 2021 — even as BSP's related Consumer Expectations Survey found household-level financial access rising to 85%, up from 74% in 2024 — suggesting access was broadening at the household level while individual account ownership and structured budgeting habits remained uneven. This mixed picture, rather than a resolved problem, remained the motivation for this study.
Several studies and reports highlighted persistent challenges in personal financial management among Filipino households. National financial inclusion surveys reported that many adults had difficulty maintaining formal budgets, tracking expenses consistently, and building emergency savings, particularly in lower-income and older age groups (Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas, 2021). These challenges were compounded by volatile prices, rising living costs, and the prevalence of informal credit arrangements, such as borrowing from relatives or neighborhood stores, which were often not recorded systematically in any tool or system (Philippine Statistics Authority, 2021; Flores, 2025).
A study on the financial freedom of Filipino workers further revealed pervasive literacy challenges, including reliance on traditional saving methods, high debt dependence, and a 'come-what-may' attitude toward financial planning — all of which contributed to weak financial resilience among Filipino households (Flores, 2025).
In the context of everyday life, parents and working adults frequently juggled multiple roles, including employment, caregiving, and managing household responsibilities. This left limited time and mental energy for manual expense recording or detailed financial planning. Traditional approaches, such as notebooks, spreadsheets, and simple expense trackers, relied heavily on user discipline and did not provide proactive feedback when spending patterns became risky. As a result, many users abandoned these tools after an initial trial period, leading to incomplete records and a lack of useful historical data for decision-making (Stefanov et al., 2024; Flores, 2025).

Globally, a variety of personal finance applications had emerged, including zero-based budgeting tools, multi-account financial dashboards, and AI-assisted expense trackers. However, many of these systems were designed for markets with widespread bank connectivity and higher levels of financial literacy, and they often depended on direct bank integration or credit card data feeds (Stefanov et al., 2024). In the Philippine context, existing applications and local solutions addressed specific aspects of financial management but still lacked integrated conversational AI, spending behavior analysis, and health scoring tailored to Filipino users who relied on mixed payment methods such as cash, e-wallets, and informal credit (Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas, 2021; Stefanov et al., 2024). At the demographic level, parents and young professionals might download budgeting apps but discontinued use when the effort of manual encoding was perceived as too high and when warnings could be ignored without visible consequences for their financial standing. Several existing applications demonstrated how mobile tools could support personal financial management but also revealed gaps that SmartSpend intended to address. Tarsi – Budget Tracker, a recently introduced application available in the Philippine App Store, supported offline expense tracking, receipt image capture, and category-based summaries, which made it suitable for users who preferred manual or semi-automatic tracking (Tarsi – Budget Tracker, 2026). However, it did not provide a conversational assistant that could interpret natural language commands, nor did it compute a unified financial health score that reflected overall budgeting behavior. YNAB (You Need A Budget) implemented a zero-based envelope budgeting method that was effective for debt payoff planning, but required manual entry and a subscription cost that might have been less accessible to Filipino users (Yomio, 2026). Monarch Money offered multi-account aggregation and net worth tracking but was designed for bank-connected markets and did not support offline functionality or Filipino locale inputs. Copilot provided AI-based expense categorization but was limited to iOS and did not support Filipino-English voice or OCR input. PocketGuard simplified available spending into a single daily figure but lacked spending behavior analysis, health scoring, or multi-modal input. These comparisons demonstrated the value of existing digital financial tools but confirmed that there was still room for a localized solution that combined offline capability, multi-modal expense input, and an accessible behavioral scoring mechanism. Beyond the applications summarized in Table 1.2, several other Philippine-context finance apps competed in this space and were acknowledged here rather than treated as a gap SmartSpend uniquely filled. Agila: Finance Coach added a business profile (invoices, inventory, collections) on top of personal tracking, with offline-first storage and no mandatory account (Agila: Finance Coach, 2026); PISO Budget Tracker offered fully offline, ad-free, payday-cycle budgeting (PISO Budget Tracker, 2026); Lista provided salary-cycle budgeting with third-party credit-score access (Lista, 2026); Kibo auto-suggested expense categories from text, receipts, or banking alerts (Kibo, 2026); and BunnyWise targeted a broader investment dashboard (PSE and US stocks, UITFs, Pag-IBIG MP2, gold, crypto) rather than day-to-day expense tracking (BunnyWise, 2026).
GCash Pera Coach, an AI financial-literacy coach embedded in the GCash e-wallet (GCash / Mynt, 2026), was a related but distinct product: it answered financial-literacy questions on demand but did not track expenses or compute a health score. None of these offered SmartSpend's combination of Taglish conversational AI, a Financial Health Score, and multi-modal (voice, OCR, barcode, batch-screenshot) input in one offline-first app, and none were put through the same hands-on test protocol as the apps in Table 1.2; they were acknowledged here so that SmartSpend's contribution would be read as an evaluated integrated prototype bundle rather than a claim of being the first or only Filipino finance app.

Building on the gaps identified in the literature and the feature comparison above, SmartSpend addressed this gap by integrating a large language model (LLM) API for natural language expense parsing, a conversational AI assistant capable of executing financial actions, and a Financial Health Score (FHS) that quantified a user's financial behavior on a scale of 0 to 100. The system operated offline using a local SQLite database with optional Firebase cloud synchronization, and supported voice, receipt OCR, barcode, and manual text AI chat, and a unified Smart Import system — including batch screenshot import that automatically detected 40+ platform types (GCash, Shopee, Steam, Lazada, GrabFood, and more) as input methods. Additionally, the application supported bulk transaction import through AI-powered text parsing, allowing users to paste transaction histories from digital wallets such as GCash and Maya, or from bank statements in various formats, for automatic parsing and categorization. It was designed for parents aged 35 to 55 and young professionals aged 21 to 35 in La Union, Philippines — two demographics identified in the literature as underserved by existing financial tools despite having significant household financial responsibilities.
Traditional financial tracking systems primarily functioned as passive tools where users manually recorded expenses and reviewed static summaries (Stefanov et al., 2024). While these systems provided basic budgeting functionalities, they often failed to offer proactive assistance, personalized recommendations, and intuitive interaction methods. As a result, users might find financial tracking tedious, leading to inconsistent usage and incomplete financial records (Stefanov et al., 2024).
Recent advancements in artificial intelligence, particularly Large Language Models (LLMs), had started changing how mobile applications handled user interaction and data processing (Davenport & Mittal, 2022; Dwivedi et al., 2021). Instead of relying only on manual input, these systems could interpret natural language and respond in a more conversational and task-oriented way (OpenAI, 2023; Hean et al., 2025). In practical terms, this allowed users to simply describe their expenses or financial goals in plain language, and the system could convert these into structured data for recording and analysis. This shift made financial tools feel less like traditional record-keeping apps and more like interactive assistants that supported daily decision-making.
This study proposed SmartSpend, a mobile financial assistant built for Android devices using Flutter (Hean et al., 2025; OpenAI, 2023). Unlike traditional budgeting apps that required users to manually encode every transaction, SmartSpend combined an LLM-based API with structured financial tracking to reduce manual work. Users could enter expenses using natural language, such as “paid 120 pesos for lunch,” and the system automatically extracted key details like amount, category, and date for storage and analysis (Hean et al., 2025; OpenAI, 2023). This design was intended to make expense tracking more natural and less time-consuming for everyday users.
Beyond expense tracking, SmartSpend featured a conversational AI assistant capable of executing real-time actions such as logging expenses, setting budgets, updating income, and creating savings goals directly from natural language commands. The system also generated a Financial Health Score ranging from 0 to 100 based on user spending patterns, consistency in budgeting, and savings behavior. Instead of only showing raw transactions, this score provided a simple reference point that helped users quickly understand whether their financial behavior was improving or declining over time. This made it easier for users to reflect on their habits without manually analyzing every expense entry.
To reduce the effort required in recording expenses, SmartSpend supported multiple input methods. Users could manually encode expenses, but they could also take photos of receipts, scan barcodes, or use voice input to record transactions. These methods were designed to fit real-life situations—for example, quickly scanning a receipt after shopping or speaking expenses while on the move. Optical Character Recognition (OCR) was used to extract text from receipts, while barcode scanning helped identify products more efficiently.
The application used local SQLite storage via the sqflite package (version 11) for offline functionality, ensuring accessibility even without an internet connection. The system implemented bidirectional cloud synchronization using Firebase Firestore, automatically pushing data to the cloud and retrieving it upon login to merge with local records. This enabled seamless multi-device access to financial data (Google, 2024c). Firebase Authentication supported secure login methods including email/password, Google Sign-In via OAuth 2.0, and biometric authentication (Google, 2024a).
Furthermore, the system analyzed financial data, generated summaries, and provided actionable insights and recommendations (Hean et al., 2025). This study contributed to financial technology by demonstrating how AI-driven automation and mobile-based interaction could enhance personal financial management (Hean et al., 2025; Dwivedi et al., 2021).
To improve accessibility and reduce barriers to adoption, SmartSpend included a Demo Mode accessible directly from the login screen without requiring account creation. This feature allowed users to explore the system using realistic sample data before registration (Bitrián et al., 2021). Additionally, the application included a guided feature tour, customizable themes, and dark mode support to enhance usability, engagement, and user satisfaction across different preferences and environments (Stefanov et al., 2024).

This figure illustrated the conceptual framework of the mobile version of the SmartSpend system, which followed the Input–Process–Output (IPO) model. The input included text input, voice input, receipt images (OCR), barcode scans, and user financial data. The process involved LLM processing, OCR extraction, barcode recognition, data structuring, and data storage and synchronization using SQLite and Firebase. These components worked together to extract, analyze, and organize financial data efficiently. The output consisted of auto-recorded expenses, financial summaries, charts, alerts, AI-generated insights, a Financial Health Score (0–100), and AI-powered financial assistance including general financial question-and-answer responses, market price estimates, and financial product guidance.
Statement of Objectives
This study aimed to design, develop, and evaluate SmartSpend, an AI-assisted mobile financial tracking and advisory application for personal financial management, with a primary focus on parents aged 35 to 55 as the main target population, and young professionals aged 21 to 35 as a secondary demographic, in La Union, Philippines.
In order to achieve the overall aim of this research, it was important to understand the key factors that influenced the money management behavior of parents and young professionals and develop a solution that addressed these challenges. This study focused on assessing the existing financial practices of parents and young professionals in La Union, designing and developing an AI-based mobile application — including the selection of a suitable large language model through comparative evaluation — that addressed identified gaps, and evaluating its usability through expert-validated instruments and standardized scoring.
Specifically, this study aimed to:
1. Assess the existing financial management practices, common budgeting challenges, and expense tracking behaviors of parents aged 35 to 55 and young professionals aged 21 to 35 in San Fernando City, La Union, through structured surveys.
2. Design and develop the SmartSpend mobile application, including its system architecture, database structure, user interface, and core functionalities such as multi-modal expense input, automated data processing, cloud-based synchronization, and the integration of a suitable Large Language Model (LLM) API — using a context injection architecture — selected through a comparative technical evaluation of available providers based on inference speed, response accuracy for Filipino-English natural language processing, context window capacity, and cost feasibility for academic deployment.
3. Evaluate the usability and performance of the SmartSpend application using the System Usability Scale (SUS) and user feedback analysis.
Scope and Limitations of the Study
As far as technology was concerned, the application supported both online and offline functionality, using a local SQLite database to store financial data and ensure accessibility even without an internet connection. The system optionally integrated cloud-based storage and authentication services through Firebase. The software utilized an LLM API to generate AI-powered insights, parse natural language inputs, and support the conversational assistant. The conversational AI assistant was also designed to respond to general financial inquiries beyond the scope of the user's recorded data, including questions about Philippine banking products, government financial services, market price estimates for goods and second-hand items, and general personal finance guidance, subject to the availability of accurate information from the underlying language model. However, the system was only available on Android devices.
The system had several limitations. The performance of OCR text recognition depended on the quality and clarity of receipt images, and handwritten or low-quality images might produce inaccurate results. Voice recognition accuracy will be affected by background noise and device support for the Filipino English (en_PH) locale. To address these issues, manual input options remained available after using OCR, barcode scanning, or voice input features. All AI-powered features, including expense parsing, financial insights, and the conversational assistant, required an active internet connection, as they relied on an external LLM API service. The application did not support direct banking integration. Bank synchronization was excluded due to the absence of accessible open-banking APIs in the Philippine setting and the data privacy implications of transmitting banking credentials to a third-party service. Investment features were outside scope as the target population primarily required support for day-to-day expense control and savings discipline rather than investment optimization. The application did not provide professional financial or tax advice. Any financial estimates generated by the system were approximations based on simplified models. Market price estimates provided by the AI assistant were based on the language model's training data and optional web search results, and might not reflect real-time pricing. Users were advised to verify prices through official sources before making financial decisions. Additionally, profile images were stored locally on the device and were not synchronized across devices due to limitations of the Firebase Spark (free) plan. The system was designed within the constraints of free-tier services and remained limited to Android platform compatibility.
The LLM API key was not embedded within the application package. Instead, it was fetched at runtime via Firebase Remote Config rather than being hard-coded into the APK. This was a prototype-stage measure only — Remote Config values were still retrievable from a decompiled client, so the key was not fully secret from a determined attacker. To mitigate potential misuse, the system enforced a daily interaction limit of 150 AI requests per user, which reset automatically. A backend proxy server for fully server-side API key management was planned as a post-capstone enhancement to further align with production-level security practices.
SmartSpend processed personal financial data and was therefore designed with the Philippine Data Privacy Act of 2012 (Republic Act No. 10173) in mind. Rather than claiming full legal compliance — which required organizational and legal measures beyond what a capstone prototype could establish — the system implemented a data-minimization control: before any OCR or pasted transaction text was sent to a cloud LLM, an on-device regex layer redacted Philippine mobile numbers, GCash/bank reference numbers, and bank account patterns, replacing them with placeholder tokens (e.g., [REDACTED_MOBILE]). Core financial records remained in the on-device SQLite database, and any Firebase Firestore sync was scoped to the authenticated user's own UID. This was a risk-reduction measure, not a guarantee of complete data protection or a certification of legal compliance.
Purpose and Description
SmartSpend was designed as a mobile application that helped users manage their daily expenses in a more organized and less manual way, addressing the core dimensions of perceived usefulness and ease of use that drove technology adoption (Davis, 1989). Instead of functioning only as a tracking tool, it also acted as a lightweight financial assistant that can respond to user inputs and provide simple insights about spending behavior.
Beyond expense tracking and data management, the SmartSpend AI assistant served as a general financial companion. Users might freely ask about current market prices of goods and second-hand items, inquire about the application process for banking products and government financial services such as SSS, PhilHealth, and Pag-IBIG, seek advice on buying and selling decisions, and explore basic financial literacy topics—all within a conversational interface that adapted to the user's needs.
The assistant was designed to be flexible and approachable, using a warm and conversational tone that reflected the everyday financial realities of Filipino users, rather than functioning as a rigid, task-only interface.
To further support healthy financial behavior, SmartSpend incorporated a behavioral intervention layer grounded in principles of behavioral finance. An Impulse Pause mechanism prompted users to reflect before confirming large Want-tagged expenses, encouraging more deliberate spending decisions. In addition, velocity alerts notified users when their spending rate significantly exceeded typical patterns. The system also included a daily mood check-in feature that tracked emotional states alongside financial activity, enabling a mood-and-spending correlation view.
A gamification layer featuring 36 achievement badges and spending streak tracking further reinforced positive financial habits by rewarding consistent logging and budget adherence. This feature helped users recognize how emotional states might influence financial decisions—an important factor in impulsive spending behavior (Bitrián et al., 2021).
SmartSpend functioned not only as an expense tracker but also as an AI-powered assistant that enhanced users’ financial literacy. The proposed solution provided benefits to the following groups:

Technical Background
In this part, the researchers described the technologies that were used during the design and development of the SmartSpend mobile application. They were critical to making the implementation of features described above possible.
Flutter. Flutter was an open-source UI framework developed by Google that was used to build the SmartSpend Android application. It provided a rich set of widgets and tools that enabled the development of a responsive, visually consistent, and feature-rich mobile interface (Google, 2024f).
SQLite. The application used SQLite as its primary local database via the sqflite package (version 11), storing all financial data including expenses, budgets, savings goals, income, recurring transactions, debts, and chat history directly on the device. Expenses were categorized across 14 expense types — including Food, Transportation, Gaming, Personal Care, Clothing, Gifts, and Travel — and tagged by payment method, supporting GCash, Maya, GrabPay, ShopeePay, debit and credit cards, and bank transfers. This ensured full offline functionality without requiring an internet connection (Roux, 2019).
Firebase Firestore. Firebase Firestore was used for bidirectional cloud synchronization of user financial data. Every write operation was automatically pushed to Firestore, and upon login, the application retrieved cloud data and merged it into the local database, enabling seamless multi-device access to financial records (Google, 2024c).
Firebase Authentication. Firebase Authentication handled user identity management, supporting email/password login, Google Sign-In via OAuth 2.0, and biometric authentication (fingerprint or face unlock) as a convenience layer after the user's first successful login (Google, 2024a).
Firebase Crashlytics. Firebase Crashlytics was integrated for real-time crash reporting. It captured Flutter framework errors and asynchronous exceptions and reported them to the Firebase Console for monitoring and debugging (Google, 2024b).
LLM API Integration. The system integrated a large language model (LLM) API as its AI engine, selected through a comparative technical evaluation of available providers based on inference speed, response accuracy for Filipino-English natural language processing, context window capacity, and cost feasibility for academic deployment. At the time of writing, Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite (Google) was the primary model, backed by seven automatic fallback providers spanning Google, Groq, and Cerebras so the assistant kept working if any single provider was unavailable or rate-limited. The full benchmarking methodology and results were presented in Chapter 3.
The system was designed with a modular LLM integration layer, meaning the underlying model could be replaced with a more capable or efficient alternative in future iterations without requiring major changes to the overall system architecture. To protect user privacy, the system minimized personally identifiable information in API requests. Only the user's natural language input and an anonymized financial summary — expense amounts, categories, and budget status — were transmitted. No authentication credentials, account numbers, or raw profile data were included in API requests, ensuring that sensitive personal information remained on the device or within the application's secure cloud storage.
This component handled multiple tasks across the application: (1) parsing natural language expense descriptions into structured data for automatic recording; (2) generating proactive spending insights and personalized financial recommendations based on the user's recorded data; (3) powering a flexible conversational AI assistant capable of answering general financial questions, providing market price estimates, explaining Philippine banking and government financial products, supporting buying and selling decisions, and executing real-time data actions — all while maintaining a warm, conversational tone appropriate for everyday Filipino financial life; and (4) periodically summarizing conversation history to maintain context efficiency without consuming excessive API tokens.
To maintain token efficiency without sacrificing conversational continuity, the system implemented a conversation summarization mechanism. Rather than sending the full chat history to the LLM API with each request, the system periodically generated a compressed summary of prior exchanges. This summary captured only financially relevant context — logged expenses, discussed goals, expressed preferences, and notable decisions — and was stored locally alongside the full chat history. Subsequent API calls received the compressed summary plus the most recent messages, significantly reducing token consumption while preserving the AI's awareness of ongoing financial discussions. The full chat history remained intact and visible to the user at all times.
Speech-to-Text. The speech_to_text package was used to implement voice input for expense recording. The system automatically detected the Filipino English (en_PH) locale, with a fallback to en_US, and supported a 15-second listening window with automatic pause detection (Sloane, 2022). 
Google ML Kit Text Recognition (OCR). Google ML Kit’s Latin script text recognizer was used to extract text from receipt images. The extracted text was cleaned, trimmed, and processed through an intelligent routing mechanism. For single-item receipts, the extracted text was passed directly to the AI chat interface for natural language parsing and automatic expense recording. For multi-item receipts, the system detected multiple line entries and routed the data to a dedicated import review screen, where each item was parsed individually using a specialized receipt parsing function. Users could review extracted entries, edit categories, assign Want or Need classifications, and selectively import items before confirming. This dual-flow design improved usability by supporting both quick single-entry recording and efficient bulk transaction processing from detailed receipts. Users had the option to capture a new image or select one from the gallery (Google, 2024e).
Mobile Scanner (Barcode). The mobile_scanner package was used for barcode scanning. Each scan was saved to a local scan history, and a prompt asked the user to describe the product before AI parsing. The scanner included a torch toggle and a visual overlay guide frame (Bociek, 2023; ZXing, 2023).
Backup and Restore. The application implemented backup and restore functionality through the system share sheet. All financial data including expenses, budgets, savings goals, income, recurring transactions, debts, wallet balances, mood log, and installment plans was exported as a structured JSON file (version 9 format) that could be shared via any installed application or saved to local storage. Restoration was performed by selecting a previously exported JSON backup file. This approach ensured data portability without requiring additional cloud service dependencies beyond the existing Firebase synchronization.
fl_chart. The fl_chart package was used to render interactive charts in the Analytics screen, including a pie chart for category breakdown, a bar chart for monthly spending trends, and a line chart for the 30-day Financial Health Score history (Flutter 4 Fun, 2022).
local_auth. The local_auth package enabled biometric authentication (fingerprint or face unlock) as a login option after the user’s first successful login, using the device’s enrolled biometrics with a PIN fallback (The Flutter Authors, 2013).
Financial Health Score (FHS). SmartSpend introduced a Financial Health Score ranging from 0 to 100 that provided users with a single, interpretable indicator of their budgeting behavior. The score operated in two modes depending on the user's settings. In Full Mode (income tracking enabled), it was computed from four equally weighted components: Savings Rate, Overspend Control, Budget Adherence, and Logging Consistency each contributing 25% to the total score. In Lightweight Mode (income tracking disabled), the four components were: Spending Restraint (vs. a user-defined spending limit), Logging Consistency, Category Balance (preventing any single category from dominating spending), and Habit Streak (consecutive days of expense logging). This dual-mode design made the system usable for individuals who did not have a fixed income, such as students and informal workers, without penalizing them for not setting income data. The detailed formula, component weights, and consequence mechanism were fully documented in Chapter 3: Results and Discussion.

Research Design
This study applied a mixed methods research design, which integrated both qualitative and quantitative approaches in collecting and analyzing data (Creswell & Plano Clark, 2011). This design was appropriate for the study because it allowed the researchers to obtain a comprehensive understanding of financial management practices and system usability by combining numerical data with detailed user experiences. Through this approach, the researchers were able to capture both measurable trends and deeper insights into user behavior, preferences, and challenges.
To address the qualitative aspect of the study, only surveys, not surveys, were used to gather respondents’ views regarding the features and functionality of the developed application. The qualitative data provided descriptive information that guided the design and improvement of the application.
On the other hand, the quantitative aspect of the study involved the use of validated structured survey questionnaires, which underwent content validation by subject matter experts to ensure clarity, relevance, and alignment with the objectives of the study. These questionnaires were used to collect measurable data regarding financial habits, expense tracking practices, and common difficulties encountered by the respondents. The collected data were analyzed to identify patterns, frequencies, and trends that supported the development of system features aligned with user needs. In addition, the System Usability Scale (SUS) questionnaire was utilized to evaluate the usability, efficiency, and overall user satisfaction of the developed SmartSpend application. The SUS was a widely used and reliable tool for assessing system usability through standardized scoring (Brooke, 1996). The SUS evaluation was administered to the thirty (30) valid respondents after respondents used the SmartSpend application (the full version or the built-in Demo Mode) for at least 10 minutes, confirmed by a screening question. A target SUS score of 80 or above, corresponding to the adjective rating of Good, was set as the acceptance threshold for this study (Bangor et al., 2009).
Moreover, this study adopted a descriptive-developmental research approach, which focused on the design, development, and evaluation of a functional system or prototype. The SmartSpend mobile application was developed using the Flutter framework, with the integration of artificial intelligence through an LLM API. This approach enabled the researchers to iteratively improve the system based on the data gathered from both qualitative and quantitative methods.
By combining mixed methods and developmental approaches, the study ensured that the system was  not only technically functional but also user-centered, effective, and aligned with the actual needs of the respondents. This integrated research design supported continuous refinement and validation of the SmartSpend application throughout the development process.

Software Methodology
Figure 2.2. Agile Kanban Workflow for SmartSpend Development
The development of the SmartSpend mobile application followed the Agile Kanban Software Development Methodology. This approach was selected because the project required continuous feature integration, testing, and refinement, especially due to the involvement of multiple components such as artificial intelligence, OCR processing, and cloud synchronization. Unlike traditional development models that followed a strict and linear sequence, Kanban allowed the researchers to work on tasks dynamically, with phases potentially overlapping or repeating as needed, while continuously improving the system based on feedback and testing results. 
To implement this methodology, the researchers utilized a Kanban board that visually represented the workflow of the project. The board was divided into several columns, namely: Backlog, Requirements, Design, Development, Testing, Deployment, and Done/Review. Each task related to the system such as implementing OCR, integrating the LLM API, or designing the database schema were represented as individual task cards. These cards moved across the board depending on their current progress. Work-in-progress (WIP) limits were also applied to ensure that the researchers focused only on a manageable number of tasks at a time, preventing overload and maintaining productivity.
In the Backlog Phase, all planned features and system requirements were first listed and organized. This included core functionalities such as expense tracking, AI-powered parsing, Financial Health Score computation, voice input integration, receipt scanning, and cloud synchronization. The researchers also included minor features such as theme customization, demo mode, and onboarding tutorials. Each feature was broken down into smaller, actionable tasks to make development more manageable.
During the Requirements Phase, the researchers gathered data through a survey of selected respondents aligned with the study’s target population of parents and young professionals. This phase aimed to identify real-world financial management practices, common challenges in expense tracking, and user expectations for a financial application. Based on the collected data, the researchers defined both functional requirements (e.g., automatic expense logging, AI recommendations) and non-functional requirements (e.g., usability, performance, offline capability).
In the Design Phase, the researchers translated the identified requirements into a concrete system structure. This included designing the application architecture, database schema, and data flow. Specifically, the SQLite database structure was mapped to handle tables such as expenses, income, budgets, savings goals, and chat history. The researchers also designed how data flowed between the mobile application, the LLM API, and Firebase services. Additionally, interface layouts and user navigation flows were planned to ensure that the application remained simple and user-friendly despite having advanced features.
The Development Phase involved the actual implementation of the system using the Flutter framework. The researchers developed the application incrementally by focusing on one feature at a time. For example, the basic expense tracking module was developed first, followed by the integration of AI parsing through the LLM API. After that, additional features such as OCR using Google ML Kit, barcode scanning, and voice input were implemented. Firebase services, including Firestore and Authentication, were also integrated during this phase to support cloud synchronization and secure login. Each completed feature was tested individually before being merged into the main application.
In the Testing Phase, the researchers conducted both functional and usability testing. Functional testing ensured that each feature operated correctly, such as verifying if the OCR accurately extracted receipt data or if the AI correctly categorized expenses. Usability testing involved selected respondents who used the application and evaluated their experience using the System Usability Scale (SUS). The researchers also observed user interactions to identify difficulties, confusion, or inefficiencies within the system. Any issues discovered during this phase were documented and fed back into the Development phase for refinement and correction.
During the Deployment Phase, the completed SmartSpend application was deployed to Android devices for actual usage and evaluation. The researchers prepared the application for demonstration and evaluation by ensuring that all features were stable and properly integrated. Demo Mode was also activated to allow users to explore the system without requiring account registration, making it easier to conduct presentations and usability testing.
Finally, in the Done/Review Phase, the researchers evaluated the overall performance and effectiveness of the system. This included analyzing SUS scores, reviewing user feedback, and assessing whether the application met its intended objectives. The researchers identified which features performed well and which areas required improvement. Insights gathered from this phase were used to refine the system and provide recommendations for future development, such as adding bank integration or improving AI accuracy. Based on the iterative development process, several features had been identified for future implementation beyond the scope of this study. A multi-wallet system enabling separate tracking of GCash, Maya, and bank balances had been fully implemented in the final version. The system supported 20 Philippine banks and 5 digital banks, plus e-wallets (GCash, Maya, GrabPay, ShopeePay, BDO, BPI, Metrobank, and more), with automatic balance deduction upon expense logging and wallet-to-wallet transfer functionality. Automated transaction parsing through Android notification listeners, while technically feasible, required sensitive permissions subject to platform policy restrictions and was therefore deferred. Additional enhancements included the implementation of a backend proxy server for secure LLM API key handling, PDF export functionality for financial summaries, and SQLite database encryption. Long-term extensions such as investment tracking for Philippine financial instruments and shared family wallet functionality would require expanded system architecture and were proposed for subsequent research.

Financial Health Score: Computation
The Financial Health Score (FHS) was SmartSpend's core academic contribution — formally positioned as a Prototype Observed Financial Health Indicator (FHI), adapting ideas from two published financial-health measurement efforts rather than inventing a scoring system from nothing. The main design choice — computing the score from a user's own transaction data instead of a survey — followed the Commonwealth Bank of Australia and Melbourne Institute's (CBA-MI) "Observed" scale, which summed categorical bank-record measures and rescaled the total to 0–100 (Comerton-Forde et al., 2018). Three of the four components below adapted the Spend, Save and Plan ideas from the Financial Health Network's FinHealth Score® (Financial Health Network, 2021). The UNSGSA's own working-group note on measuring financial health treated transaction/account-based measurement as a promising but still early-stage fourth approach, better viewed as complementary to survey-based scales rather than a full replacement (Gubbins, Mazzotta & Rhyne, 2021) — which was exactly why this score was named a "Prototype," not presented as a validated instrument in its own right.
Equal weights that added up to 100 followed the simplest published scoring method for this kind of scale. CBA's own 10-question Reported scale gave each question equal weight (0–4 points, 40 total) and multiplied by 2.5 to land on a 0–100 range, and separate validation testing found that this simple approach correlated very highly with more complex model-based scores (Gubbins, Mazzotta & Rhyne, 2021). SmartSpend followed the same logic with four components: 4 × 25 = 100. The weights themselves were a design choice, not a statistically derived result — this was stated plainly rather than implied to be more rigorous than it was.

(1) Cloud AI (default): requests go through the provider chain, with mobile numbers, reference numbers and bank-account patterns redacted on-device first. The primary provider was used under Google's free ("unpaid") Gemini API quota, whose terms stated that submitted content might be used to improve Google's products, might be read by human reviewers, and should not include personal information; redaction therefore reduced but did not remove the exposure, and users with sensitive details were directed to Private Local Mode. Since v3.0.5 a one-time privacy disclosure was also shown before the first AI message. (2) Private Local Mode (implemented, v2.9.92): while the app could reach a model running on the user's own PC or Mac over the home network, requests went only to that model and financial data did not leave the network; if the local server was unreachable the app fell back to the cloud chain, unless the user had switched on Local-only mode (added in v3.0.4), in which case the AI stopped responding instead of falling back. (3) On-device AI (planned, not implemented): a model running on the phone itself, with no network at all. Research at the time indicated this was feasible on 2026 flagship phones and, as an opt-in download of roughly 2.6 GB, on phones with about 6 GB of RAM, but not yet on the 4 GB mid-range phones common among Filipino students; it was therefore listed under Recommendations, not as a delivered feature.
From v3.0.5 to v3.1.0 the app's mascot, Peso, was developed from a static icon into a context-aware companion. The mascot has 12 animated moods (idle movement, periodic blinking, animated mood changes, tap reactions and an optional speech bubble), appears on most major screens and as the avatar beside AI replies, and changes mood with the sentiment of the assistant's reply and, on the home screen, with the Financial Health Score tier. A voice service supplies short Taglish messages for 31 situations (two to four variants each, chosen at random so the same line does not repeat every session) used in the AI greeting, companion messages and the app's scheduled notifications; a context service gives a one-time tip per screen per session for 17 screens; and a haptic service provides tactile feedback. Once-a-day checks react to events such as no expenses logged by 2 PM, a no-spend day, budget status, upcoming bills, a change in FHS tier, logging streaks of 7, 14, 21 and 30 days, and savings-goal milestones at 25, 50, 75 and 100 percent, and ten additional achievement badges (36 in total) were added with celebration sheets. Tapping a notification stores its payload so that the next launch can respond in context.
These behaviours applied the same ideas used for Warning Decay and the badge system — prompt, non-blocking, autonomy-supportive feedback tied to the user's own logging and score changes (Thaler & Sunstein, 2008; Deci & Ryan, 2000). They were added after the usability-evaluation design was fixed, so their effect on user behaviour has not been measured; the longitudinal study proposed in Chapter IV was where it would be tested. Present Peso as a design feature, not as evidence of behaviour change, and avoid phrases such as "dopamine reinforcement".
The optional local-LLM integration described above shipped with a guided in-app setup flow covering three common local-inference tools (Ollama, LM Studio, Jan), including a hardware-tier picker (8/16/32GB RAM) to help a non-technical user choose a workable local model size, and fell back to the cloud provider chain if the local server became unreachable mid-session, unless the user has switched on Local-only mode.

CHAPTER IV
CONCLUSIONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS
This chapter presented the conclusions drawn from the results of the study and the recommendations of the researchers.
Conclusions
SmartSpend v3.1.0 was developed as a working Android application with multi-modal expense input, offline-first local storage, 34 AI actions, and a Financial Health Score formally classified as a Prototype Observed Financial Health Indicator. The comparative evaluation of 20 candidate LLM models led to the selection of Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite as the primary model, supported by a multi-provider failover chain. Therefore, the second objective of designing and developing the SmartSpend mobile application, including the selection of a suitable LLM API, was achieved.

Recommendations
In view of the results of the study, the following recommendations were made:
1. Payday-cycle-aware budgeting. The researchers recommended that the developers implement budgeting resets aligned with the semi-monthly (15th and 30th) salary cycle common in the Philippines.
2. Backend API proxy and per-user keys. The researchers recommended that the developers move LLM API key management to a server-side proxy (e.g., Firebase Cloud Function) to remove device-side key exposure and enforce per-user message limits, because the free-tier request quotas (Table 3.2) were shared by every user of the prototype. Letting users add their own key was an interim measure.
3. Google Play release. The researchers recommended that, after the backend proxy and a privacy policy were in place, the developers submit  SmartSpend to the Google Play Store, since the prototype was currently distributed as a downloadable APK.
4. On-device AI tier. The researchers recommended that the developers add  an opt-in on-device model (e.g., Gemma 4 E2B through Google's LiteRT-LM, about a 2.6 GB download) for users who wanted fully offline AI, routing only simple logging tasks to it and keeping complex financial advice on the cloud tier, because the current local AI mode required a separate computer on the same network.
5. SQLite encryption. The researchers recommended that the developers implement SQLCipher-based encryption for the local database in a future schema migration, because the local database of the prototype was not encrypted.
6. Household and remittance features. The researchers recommended that the developers add  shared couple and family wallets and inbound OFW remittance tracking as a distinct income category to serve more user groups.
7. Longitudinal evaluation. The researchers recommended that future researchers measure  SmartSpend's effect on financial behavior over three to six months with a larger sample, because the usability evaluation in this study measured usability at a single point in time.
